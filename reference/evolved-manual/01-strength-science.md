## The Eight Physical Assets of Longevity

The Evolved Standards are not designed to determine who is the strongest woman in the room. They are designed to answer a more important question: **what should a woman be physically capable of doing if she wants to live a long, healthy, independent life?**

Every test within the Evolved Standards Framework exists because it measures one or more of eight physical assets that influence how well a woman ages. The standards themselves are not the goal. The physical assets they represent are.

---

### Asset 1 — Strength

Strength is the foundation of physical capability.

Without strength:
- Everyday tasks become harder
- Falls become more dangerous
- Recovery becomes slower
- Independence becomes increasingly fragile

Strength allows a woman to lift children, carry luggage, move furniture, climb stairs, and recover from trips and slips. It is the non-negotiable base on which every other physical asset depends.

**Measured through:** Deadlift, Squat, Bench Press, Overhead Press, Lat Pulldown

---

### Asset 2 — Muscle Mass

Muscle is the organ of longevity.

Muscle influences metabolism, blood sugar regulation, hormonal health, bone density, injury resilience, and healthy ageing. Beginning around age 30, women naturally lose muscle unless they actively work to maintain it — **3–8% of muscle mass per decade**, accelerating through menopause (see: sarcopenia below).

Every kilogram of muscle preserved helps protect future health. Strength training at adequate intensity is the primary evidence-based intervention to maintain and build muscle mass.

**Measured through:** Relative strength standards, push-ups, pull-ups, functional strength standards

**Sarcopenia:** The age-related loss of muscle mass. Begins in the 30s, accelerates through menopause. Compounds into reduced metabolic rate, decreased bone density, hormonal disruption, and loss of functional independence. Strength training at the right parameters is the single most effective intervention.

---

### Asset 3 — Bone Density

Strong bones reduce the risk of fractures, osteoporosis, and loss of independence.

Bone responds to load. The body builds stronger bones when it is required to handle meaningful resistance. Women are at significantly higher risk of osteoporosis than men — estrogen decline through perimenopause and menopause accelerates bone loss. Strength training is the most accessible and effective intervention.

**Measured through:** Squats, deadlifts, split squats, sled work, loaded carries

---

### Asset 4 — Mobility

Mobility determines whether strength can actually be used.

Many people lose independence not because they are weak but because they can no longer access the positions required for life. Getting up from the floor. Reaching overhead. Climbing stairs. Stepping over obstacles. Mobility is not flexibility for its own sake — it is usable range of motion under control.

**Measured through:** ATG Split Squat, Pistol Squat, Overhead Press, functional assessments

---

### Asset 5 — Balance & Coordination

Most falls occur while standing on one leg — not two.

Balance and coordination become increasingly important with age. The ability to stabilise, react, and control movement can significantly reduce fall risk — one of the primary causes of injury-related loss of independence in women over 60.

**Measured through:** Reverse lunges, Pistol Squats, single-arm loading, split squats

---

### Asset 6 — Work Capacity

Work capacity determines how much life the body can tolerate before fatigue becomes the limiting factor.

Walking through airports. Exploring new cities. Playing with grandchildren. Completing long days of physical activity. A woman with high work capacity is not limited by her fitness — her energy and endurance match her ambition.

**Measured through:** Running standards, rowing standards, beep test, sled work, Farmer Carries

---

### Asset 7 — Independence

Independence is the ultimate outcome.

Strength. Muscle. Mobility. Balance. Fitness. All of them exist to support one thing: freedom. The ability to continue doing the things you love without relying on others. The ability to remain capable. The ability to choose activity rather than avoid it.

**Measured through:** Farmer Carries (Walk of Independence), dead hangs, running, stair climbing, functional strength standards

---

### Asset 8 — Grip Strength & Carrying Capacity

Most people think independence is lost suddenly. In reality, it is usually lost gradually.

A missed step. A minor fall. Difficulty carrying groceries. Struggling to lift luggage. Needing assistance to move everyday objects. Over time, these small limitations accumulate until they begin restricting how a person lives.

Leading longevity experts including Dr Peter Attia, Dr Gabrielle Lyon, and Dr Stacy Sims regularly emphasise the importance of maintaining grip strength and carrying capacity throughout life. Research has consistently shown that grip strength is one of the strongest predictors of:

- Frailty
- Disability
- Loss of independence
- Falls
- All-cause mortality

At The Evolved, we measure this through our signature standard: **The Walk of Independence.**

| Level | Standard |
|---|---|
| **Live** | 75% of bodyweight carried for 1 minute |
| **Long** | 100% of bodyweight carried for 1 minute |
| **Perform** | 150% of bodyweight carried for 1 minute |

This standard was selected because it simultaneously challenges grip strength, core stability, postural endurance, lower body strength, work capacity, and mental resilience.

Most importantly, it reflects a quality that remains essential throughout life: **the ability to carry your own body and your own burdens.**

Groceries. Suitcases. Children. Grandchildren. Life.

A woman who maintains meaningful carrying strength into her seventies and eighties is far more likely to remain active, capable, and independent than one who does not.

**Measured through:** Farmer Carries (Walk of Independence), all loaded carry variations

## How This Framework Shapes Coaching

When a client walks in with a weight loss goal, a coach who understands the seven assets can connect her goal to a deeper outcome: the body composition she wants is a byproduct of the muscle mass and metabolic function she's building. When she builds strength, she builds bone density. When she builds work capacity, she can keep up with her kids at 50 and her grandchildren at 70.

This framework is not a sales tool. It is the honest, evidence-based explanation of why structured strength training matters more than any other physical activity a woman can pursue.

---

## The Key Performance Habits

The KPH are the minimum training parameters required to drive meaningful adaptation across all seven assets.

**Priority 1 — Training Intensity**

| Parameter | Target |
|---|---|
| Rep range | 6–12 RM |
| Intensity | 70–85% of 1RM |
| Volume | 6–8 sets per session |

**Priority 2 — Movement Pattern Coverage**

**Squat · Hinge · Lunge · Push · Pull · Carry**

Each movement pattern maps to one or more physical assets. Carry develops grip strength and independence. Hinge builds posterior chain strength and bone density. Together, the six patterns ensure balanced development across all seven assets.

---

## The Direction of Progress

The standards are not pass or fail. They are directional.

A woman who improves her deadlift from 50% of bodyweight to 100% has dramatically improved her future physical capacity. A woman who progresses from a 30-second plank to 2 minutes has improved her resilience. A woman who progresses from struggling with stairs to climbing multiple flights comfortably has improved her quality of life.

Every improvement increases capability. Every improvement builds resilience. Every improvement future-proofs the body.

The standards provide a map. The journey toward them is where the real transformation occurs.

## Coach Scripts

### Scenario: Client asks "Why do I need to lift heavy?"

> *"Because your body only builds what it needs. If you give it light resistance, it only builds enough muscle to handle light resistance. From your 30s onward, your body is actively losing muscle — 3–8% per decade — unless you give it a strong enough reason to keep it. The rep ranges and loads we use are the minimum stimulus required to drive adaptation. Below that threshold, training feels like exercise but doesn't produce lasting change."*

### Scenario: Client wants to do cardio instead of strength

> *"Cardio feels harder in the moment — but it doesn't produce the same outcomes. Strength training is the primary evidence-based intervention for muscle mass, bone density, metabolic rate, and hormonal health. Cardio doesn't move those dials the same way. For your specific goals — [insert their goal] — strength training is the most effective path. Can we try it our way and see how it feels?"*
